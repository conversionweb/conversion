Temperature converter — Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin

Temperature is the one family where units do not simply scale — each has its own zero — so this converter takes a single value and rewrites it on every scale, including the two historical ones.

Exact conversion factors

These are the definitions the calculator uses. Nothing here is rounded for convenience — where a value is exact by definition, it says so.

  • 0 °C = 32 °F = 273.15 K — water freezes
  • 100 °C = 212 °F = 373.15 K — water boils at 1 atm
  • −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F = 0 K — absolute zero
  • −40 °C = −40 °F — the one point where both scales agree
  • 37 °C = 98.6 °F — normal body temperature

How this converter works

Pick the scale you are reading from, type the number, and the rest fill in. Negative values, decimals and the awkward ones like −40 (identical in Celsius and Fahrenheit) all work.

The arithmetic, if you want to do it by hand: °F = °C × 9/5 + 32, and K = °C + 273.15. Rankine is the Fahrenheit-sized cousin of Kelvin, used in some US thermodynamics work; Réaumur is a historical scale with water boiling at 80° that still turns up in old recipes and cheese-making instructions.

Notes and abbreviations

  • 0 °C = 32 °F = 273.15 K — water freezes
  • 100 °C = 212 °F = 373.15 K — water boils at sea level
  • −273.15 °C = 0 K — absolute zero, the floor of the Kelvin scale
  • −40 °C = −40 °F — the single point where the two scales agree

Questions

What is a fever of 38.5 °C in Fahrenheit?

101.3 °F. Normal body temperature, 36.6 °C, is 97.88 °F.

Is it 273 or 273.15?

The exact offset is 273.15. The rounded 273 is fine for rough work but drifts in precise calculations.

What oven temperature is gas mark 6?

About 200 °C, which this page shows as 392 °F and 473.15 K.
